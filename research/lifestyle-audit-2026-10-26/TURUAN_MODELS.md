# Turuan mental models for fitness that fits real life

Reviewed: 2026-10-26. Scope: editorial research and proposed adaptations, not a production change or a validated intervention.

**Owner clarification, 27 October:** use this as an optional reference library. Do not require mental models, a model field or the three recurring moves proposed below in any episode. Start from the viewer's problem and use a model only when it makes the explanation naturally simpler. The casual voice and existing Blueprint identity take precedence.

## Recommendation

Use Turuan as a library for making fitness decisions simpler. Keep model names in the production notes; show viewers the situation, the decision and one usable next step. A viewer should leave knowing what to do after work, not needing to learn another framework.

For this month, introduce only three recurring moves: **choose what fits, prepare a fallback, and return after disruption**. The five source models below support those moves plus progress review. We do not need five new branded series or a mental-model lesson in every Short.

The current calendar already has the right topics. Its opportunity is to turn general advice into a worked example. BP04 already suggests a familiar walking route and an indoor backup; BP08 already asks viewers to schedule realistic training days; BP16 already asks for one barrier and one adjustment. Make those actions visible and specific before adding more concepts.

## Five useful models, translated into fitness decisions

### 1. The Dimmer Switch Mentality

- **Exact ID:** `106-dimmer-switch-mentality`.
- **Database:** [record](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/zz_data/mental_models.json:3856).
- **Narration:** [source script](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/01_scripts/106-dimmer-switch-mentality.md).
- **Status:** editorial metaphor. The database attributes the framing to Sahil Bloom; it is not a validated exercise prescription.
- **Viewer version:** “Give your plan a busy-week version.”
- **Best fit:** BP04 walking, BP08 two training days, BP16 missed workout, BPK04 realistic-week carousel.

**Worked example:** Show an after-lunch outdoor walking plan and a practical indoor alternative when weather changes. End with a fill-in card: “After ___, I can walk at ___. If that is unavailable, my backup is ___.” The viewer chooses an appropriate duration and route; this card does not prescribe a universal dose.

For lifting, show a routine's own shorter-session or rescheduling guidance where available. Do not tell a beginner to invent a random abbreviated workout. If the routine offers no suitable alternative, checking how to adapt it is itself the next action.

**Avoid:** “Never switch fitness off,” guilt about rest, and claims that a tiny action preserves all fitness. Turuan's script makes broad claims about small actions preventing deterioration across life domains. Those claims are not imported into fitness copy. A walk can be useful movement without being equivalent to a missed strength session; recovery, illness and other constraints can justify stopping.

### 2. Rocks, Pebbles & Sand

- **Exact ID:** `124-rocks-pebbles-sand`.
- **Database:** [record](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/zz_data/mental_models.json:4342).
- **Narration:** [source script](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/01_scripts/124-rocks-pebbles-sand.md).
- **Status:** prioritization parable. Turuan's source notes associate it with Stephen R. Covey's *First Things First*; the record itself links a Sahil Bloom newsletter.
- **Viewer version:** “Choose the few things your actual week can support.”
- **Best fit:** BP01 first visit, BP08 two training days, BP14 sleep, BPK04 weekly planning.

**Worked example:** Start with a calendar that already includes work, care and social commitments. Place the sessions from a suitable two-day beginner routine into genuinely available slots. Include travel and changing time. Leave slack rather than filling every remaining minute. End: “Put your next session in the space you actually have.”

The brand's 80/20 idea becomes a prioritization question: “Which decision matters most right now?” For a new viewer, that might be choosing a suitable routine and learning setup before buying supplements or fine-tuning an advanced split.

**Avoid:** saying the 80/20 ratio guarantees 80% of fitness results from 20% of effort; treating care work or relationships as distractions; promising everything fits if arranged correctly. Turuan's script includes a forceful claim that the issue is order rather than time. We should retain its later acknowledgment that some people do not control their schedule and put that realism in the example itself.

### 3. List Price vs. Real Price

- **Exact ID:** `032-list-price-vs-real-price`.
- **Database:** [record](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/zz_data/mental_models.json:1541).
- **Narration:** [source script](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/01_scripts/032-list-price-vs-real-price.md).
- **Status:** editorial application of opportunity cost; not evidence that proximity alone causes adherence.
- **Viewer version:** “Count the whole trip, not just the workout.”
- **Best fit:** BP07 run club, BP08 scheduling, BP11 lunch, BP15 trying an activity.

**Worked example:** Illustrative numbers: a 30-minute class plus 15 minutes travelling each way plus 10 minutes changing takes 70 minutes. A less glamorous nearby option might fit better. Compare actual cost, travel, beginner help and enjoyment together, rather than automatically picking the cheapest or nearest choice.

End BP15 with: “Before booking, check the door-to-door time and whether the session supports beginners.” For lunch, use the lighter version: check ingredients, cooking time and cleanup before choosing an ambitious meal-prep recipe.

**Avoid:** turning every lunch into an elaborate optimization exercise, implying convenience is everyone's only constraint, or claiming these illustrative numbers are typical. Retain the source model's distinction between seeing costs clearly and rejecting every activity because it has costs.

### 4. The One-in-a-Row Principle

- **Exact ID:** `165-one-in-a-row-principle`.
- **Database:** [record](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/zz_data/mental_models.json:5506).
- **Narration:** [source script](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/01_scripts/165-one-in-a-row-principle.md).
- **Status:** productivity aphorism, attributed in the source notes to Sahil Bloom's popularization. It is not a tested treatment for exercise adherence.
- **Viewer version:** “Plan the return, not the perfect streak.”
- **Best fit:** BP16 missed workout; secondary use in BP01 orientation and BP11 choosing one meal.

**Worked example:** A Tuesday session is missed because work runs late. Show the routine's guidance, the next suitable session and a calendar entry. The action is to choose that return visit and remove one identifiable barrier, such as packing the bag earlier. Do not automatically prescribe doubling the next workout or changing its training volume.

End: “Write your next realistic visit: day, place, and the first thing you need ready.” This creates a usable response to a lapse. It is stronger than an abstract instruction to be consistent.

**Avoid:** promising one tiny action creates a habit, presenting a streak as moral achievement, or saying the size of the goal is always the main reason someone struggles. Injury, access, money and competing responsibilities can be material barriers.

### 5. Goodhart's Law

- **Exact ID:** `145-goodharts-law`.
- **Database:** [record](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/zz_data/mental_models.json:4966).
- **Narration:** [source script](/Users/ronobdin/Github/turuan/01_scripts/145-goodharts-law.md).
- **Status:** a warning about optimizing a proxy, adapted editorially. Turuan notes the distinction between Goodhart's original work and Strathern's later phrasing; that provenance is not a fitness study.
- **Viewer version:** “Track something that helps your next decision.”
- **Best fit:** BP06 soreness, BP10 one bad workout, BP13 month-one scorecard.

**Worked example:** Keep a small training log containing exercise/setup, load and reps. At a weekly review, ask what became easier to execute and what practical obstacle needs attention. This extends BP13's existing visits/setup/log scorecard without adding an elaborate dashboard.

Show what the measure establishes: attendance records visits; a setup note records a skill; comparable training entries help review performance. None alone proves muscle growth or guarantees a particular body change.

**Avoid:** dismissing all measurements, adding ten trackers, or making a categorical claim that hitting a step goal has no benefit. The Turuan script uses examples of metrics becoming unhelpful; those rhetorical examples must not become unsupported fitness claims.

## Evidence: useful behavioral ideas still need context

The five selected database records all link to Sahil Bloom editorial material. Their `sources.json` entries mostly provide provenance descriptions, not clinical or behavioral-study records. The reusable metaphors should therefore be labeled as editorial tools. Research supporting a related technique does not validate the named metaphor, our exact script, or a short social-media exposure.

1. **Simple scheduling is not sufficient evidence of behavior change.** Carrera and colleagues randomized 877 gym members to make attendance plans or report prior attendance. The planning intervention did not increase subsequent gym attendance. This is a direct reason to avoid “put it in your calendar and consistency follows.” [Primary study, *Journal of Health Economics*, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7872013/).

2. **More specific planning can be promising in some settings.** A randomized pilot with 63 adults aged 35–69 used schedules and maps to plan where, when and how to add walking. It reported increases in activity relative to control. This small, older sample and supported intervention do not establish the effects of a Reel among male beginners aged 18–35. [Primary trial, *Psychology & Health*, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6440859/).

3. **Returning after a missed workout is worth testing, not overclaiming.** A gym megastudy involving 61,293 members compared 54 four-week programs. A leading intervention rewarded a return after a missed workout, as part of a package that included other supports. This does not show that the wording “one in a row,” a supportive character, or a no-cost encouragement alone causes adherence. [Primary study, *Nature*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8822539/).

The editorial inference is to test concrete planning, realistic barriers and restart cues. We should measure whether viewers understand and use the suggestion, rather than claiming a scientifically proven habit system.

## Incorporate without complicating

Use one structure across relevant clips:

1. **Recognizable interruption:** the meeting ran late; the gym is farther away than expected; tomorrow's lunch is unplanned.
2. **One visible decision:** choose a realistic slot, a suitable backup, or the next return.
3. **One completed example:** show the filled calendar, route card, shopping note or training-log entry.
4. **One audience action:** invite the viewer to make their own version immediately.

Keep model provenance in captions or production notes when useful. The phrase “mental model” does not need to appear on screen. The explanatory graphic should illustrate the decision; it should not require viewers to decode a new diagram and learn a new term before receiving the benefit.

A practical first test is **BP04**: existing broad time-and-route guidance versus a worked “after lunch / route / weather backup” example. **BP16** can then test whether a worked restart plan clarifies what to do after a missed visit. Preserve the factual boundary and the same general scope. Treat these as pilot revisions unless the owner chooses a broader production update.

## Test whether it helps

Before changing the month, show the two versions to a small group of relevant beginners/returners, randomizing which version they see first. Ask without prompting: “What would you do next?”, “When or where would you do it?”, and “What does this video not establish?” Record whether they give a feasible action, need explanation, or mistake a backup for equivalent training. This is qualitative usability evidence, not a causal effectiveness trial.

On platform, track comprehension feedback, saves, substantive replies and retention alongside the existing audience-interest measures. A voluntary later reply reporting use is useful directional evidence but remains self-reported and selected. Saves or watch time alone do not demonstrate increased activity. If a model improves recall but makes the action harder to explain, simplify it.

For sustainability, record the extra scripting, illustration and revision time. A reused calendar/route/log template is a better first test than creating a distinct visual world for every model. The existing twelve-hour weekly limit remains the production constraint.

## Do not import

- Turuan's long-form seven-section structure or its 1,600-word target into short-form fitness.
- Its named-model intro, fixed long-form subscription close or performance claims into this brand without adaptation.
- A mandate that the goal never changes. People's goals, health and responsibilities can legitimately change.
- The “85% rule,” “80/10 test” or other numeric heuristics as exercise intensity, effort or nutrition prescriptions.
- Unverified author attributions as scientific evidence, or generic productivity examples as medical advice.

The strongest contribution is a simpler action inside an existing fitness lesson. The brand remains about a body and life the viewer values; the models help them make the next practical decision.
