# Character test: does aspiration earn useful attention?

Protocol CT1 · retired by owner on 22 October 2026 without participant testing. Historical design only; not a current launch gate. This supplements the month-one protocol; it does not replace its interest, comprehension or 12-hour production gates. No participant outreach, paid recruitment or publication has occurred. Prior V4.0 stimuli and hashes are historical. Current V4.1 removes the character and provides a separate citation comparison.

## Question and arms

Does visible muscularity in our fictional guide improve voluntary viewing for adult male beginners, without reducing understanding, trust or confidence that they can take the next step?

| Arm | What the viewer sees | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| M | Visibly muscular, approachable guide | Candidate for aspiration |
| R | Same guide, less muscular definition | Candidate for greater perceived similarity |
| G | Training Club graphics without a character | Whether adding a character is useful at all |

M versus R is the primary physique comparison. G is a secondary production baseline. R is not labeled “the average male,” “unfit” or “the beginner.” Participants see neutral file labels; we measure whether either character feels similar to them.

## Stimulus contract

Use the final B04 notation explanation and B08 two-day planning explanation as two topic blocks. Create three variants of each: six short stimuli. Reuse their approved narration and timing. M/R must preserve face, apparent adult age, skin tone, hair, clothing coverage/color, height, expression, gesture, role, screen position, screen time and render quality. Change muscle volume/definition only; avoid simultaneously changing body fat, posture, apparent confidence or beauty treatment. Review remaining imperfections as limitations.

No flexing, shirtless reveal, body transformation or fake autobiographical dialogue. Both guides explain a task; neither is introduced as an expert or a novice. Any fictional-character disclosure is identical in M/R. Give both the same evidence links and cues. G uses the same script, timing, captions, graphics and composition reserved for the illustration. Its blank/graphic region makes G a package comparison, not a pure muscularity manipulation.

Use exactly the same rendered audio bytes, captions, headline, frame rate and export resolution within each topic. Preserve the explanatory object at the same size rather than shrinking it to make room for a body. Keep source cues fixed during CT1. Do not also test three brand styles, different hooks or different voices.

## Phase 0: manipulation and readability check

Use 6–9 adult beginners who will not join the main screen. Rotate which M/R version they see first. After individual ratings, allow comparison. Confirm that M is perceived as more muscular while the identity, age, friendliness, clothing and production quality remain comparable. Collect perceived similarity and attainability; do not assume R wins them. Check phone readability and whether attention shifts away from the explanatory object. Repeated unintended differences trigger a revision before freezing files.

Record exact video hashes and export receipts. If the artwork cannot isolate muscle size, label the test a comparison of those two character packages; do not later claim a pure causal effect of muscularity.

## Phase 1: 36-person feasibility screen

Recruit self-identified men aged 18–35 who report at most six months of consistent resistance-training experience. Aim for 18 aged 18–24 and 18 aged 25–35. These are pragmatic quotas, not a representative sample. An adult screen does not validate use with 16–17-year-olds. No body photos, weight, body-fat estimates or health histories are needed.

Within each age band, use three shuffled blocks of the six arm × topic combinations. Assign in enrollment order after eligibility, retain the allocation log, and do not reassign after seeing a response. This yields 12 people per arm, with six on each topic; total n = 36. Each person sees one assigned version first on a phone. They can stop or skip without losing any agreed compensation. Do not tell them that muscles are the study's focus before the first exposure.

**Primary outcome:** first-video completion, defined as playing at least 90% of unique media time while the video is visible. Ignore replayed seconds and background-tab playback. Report all assigned participants, starts, skips, dropouts and technical failures; a proven playback failure is missing, not lack of interest. Preserve an assignment-based conservative sensitivity count with unobserved completion treated as not completed. Do not use a moderator's impression of attention as telemetry.

After the viewing opportunity, ask the unaided comprehension questions, then the brief ratings in the field guide. Finally offer the same optional next explanation with no autoplay. Its actual voluntary start is a secondary behavior; record that it occurred after the survey. Side-by-side aesthetic preference comes last, outside the first-exposure comparison.

**Secondary outcomes:** fraction viewed; optional next-video start; takeaway/action/limitation pass; trust; task confidence; inspiration; perceived similarity; perceived attainability; discouragement; remembered brand. These explain a possible mechanism but are not ten opportunities to pick a flattering winner. Record mute state and playback problems. Compare M–R first, then each against G, with counts by topic and age band; the small subgroups are descriptive.

## Decision rules and uncertainty

This 36-person screen finds broken stimuli and large directional differences; it cannot establish a reliable retention winner. At 12 per arm, one person changes a rate by 8.3 percentage points. Report uncertainty and raw counts, not just percentages. Do not stop enrollment early because the favored character leads.

Advance a character only if its stimulus manipulation works, at least 10 of its 12 viewers pass the comprehension rubric, no same material misunderstanding recurs unresolved in two people, and viewing/next-step measures provide a reason to test further. Two independently reported character-related discouragement problems trigger inspection and repair; this is a product stop rule, not a clinical measure. If both character arms offer no useful signal over G, keep graphics-only and its lower production cost. If M and R trade aspiration against confidence, revise a specific feature or retain the current baseline; do not declare a winner from a weighted score invented afterward.

If a commercial causal claim is worth the extra work, run a fresh, fixed two-arm M/R confirmation. A planning example is approximately 100 people per arm to detect a large completion change from 50% to 70% at 80% power and two-sided alpha .05 under a standard two-proportion normal approximation. This is not a forecast; smaller effects need substantially more participants. Freeze the minimum worthwhile difference, sample size, exclusions and analysis before collecting confirmation data. Use a new cohort, split topics evenly, and analyze uncertainty for M–R. A 36-person lead alone does not select the full-month character.

Any eventual adoption must also pass the existing brand/comprehension rules and measured production capacity. Track asset creation separately from per-episode posing, animation, review and revisions. The complete four-video weekly workflow still must fit 12 hands-on hours. A rendered avatar is not automatically a sustainable asset.

## Source-citation test: separate follow-up

After holding the character choice fixed (or using G), compare B03 with source links in the description versus the identical clip with the added readable label “Schoenfeld et al., 2016 · 21 trained men.” Keep the existing spoken scope in both. This tests added on-screen citation visibility, not the value of having sources at all. First inspect whether the label competes with captions; do not shrink the claim or alter voice speed.

A separate 24–36-person screen can assess noticing, credibility, comprehension and readability. It is exploratory, not powered to detect the small effect found in trivia research. Use fresh participants, balanced first exposure and one preselected primary trust question; retain all ratings. A null result does not justify removing accessible sources. Do not combine this with CT1 into six cells of six participants and call the interaction established.
