# Writing an irresistible promise that the video actually keeps

Our version of “almost clickbait” is **specific tension with an honest payoff**. We can make an ordinary question feel immediately relevant without exaggerating the evidence or withholding the useful answer. Headline research supports common words and manageable information gaps; it does not establish a magic word list for fitness Shorts. [Simple writing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11152133/), [curiosity gaps](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-81575-9)

## Start with a decision, then write the hook

Write the answer in one plain sentence before writing the hook. Next name the object or situation the viewer recognizes. Then choose the tension: confusion, a surprising comparison, an unnecessary purchase, or a solvable constraint. If the answer needs five qualifications to be responsible, narrow the claim instead of speeding through the caveats.

| Weak or misleading version | Our version | Why this is a better contract |
|---|---|---|
| “This one gym secret changes everything.” | “First gym visit—what do you do first?” | Names the viewer’s situation |
| “Machines are killing your gains.” | “Machines can be a valid place to start.” | Makes a bounded, supportable claim |
| “Stop wasting time between sets.” | “Are you rushing your next set?” | Opens a question without declaring rest waste |
| “You’re doing 3 × 8 wrong.” | “Three by eight means three separate sets.” | Pays off a visible notation problem |
| “No soreness? No growth.” | “Soreness is not your progress score.” | Removes a misleading rule |
| “Double your gains with this trick.” | “Same weight, one extra rep: progress?” | Uses a concrete comparison without a fabricated effect |
| “You need this supplement.” | “Protein powder is optional equipment for your diet.” | Reframes necessity; final script uses simpler wording |
| “You failed your routine.” | “Missed a workout? Plan the next one.” | Names an action instead of an identity judgment |

The replacement lines are original editorial examples. Only the selected canonical narration is synthesized; unscheduled alternatives are not additional released videos.

## Six-beat script, flexible timing

**Hook:** one recognizable problem. **Answer:** useful information before a long setup. **Example:** a visible change or concrete scenario. **Boundary:** the limitation that matters. **Action:** one thing to do. **Close:** a relevant question. This structure is a production heuristic. The final measured audio, rather than a words-per-minute guess, determines timing.

Write mostly one idea per sentence. Prefer “hard set” to “high-intensity stimulus,” “what you record” to “tracking infrastructure,” and “same setup” to “standardized testing conditions.” Define a necessary term immediately. Numbers need a job: “three groups of eight” teaches notation; an unexplained “optimal 17.3 sets” creates false precision. Speak a numerical example in words while showing the digits.

The narrator is explaining, not performing certainty. Use contractions when they sound natural. Read the text aloud conceptually and remove tongue-twisting clauses before synthesis. Avoid repeating the full headline, a long evidence label and the same sentence in three visual places. Full captions remain available; the explanatory graphic should add meaning.

## Question versus answer openings

Each episode has both an original question opening and a direct-answer alternative. The month schedules eight of each, balanced across week, topic cluster and weekday. The alternate stays unscheduled. This gives us a disciplined exploratory comparison, but different topics and audiences mean it is not randomized causal evidence.

Do not call a question “better” just because it produces more comments. It may attract confusion. Compare mature attention metrics, substantive responses and comprehension. The end question asks about a process or constraint, not a confession about appearance.

## The evidence-to-script editing pass

1. Underline every factual claim, including the hook and visual labels.
2. Identify its source and population. Mark editorial advice as editorial advice.
3. Check whether the spoken boundary prevents the obvious overgeneralization.
4. Check that a muted viewer sees a compatible claim in the graphic and captions.
5. Ask a beginner to explain the takeaway without replaying.
6. Verify the final speech and captions preserve the approved words; log unresolved ASR differences for listening.

Examples of necessary boundaries: adult research is not an adolescent prescription; a comparison log is illustrative, not a trial result; a machine is not interchangeable with every other exercise; one bad session cannot diagnose a plateau. No generic disclaimer can compensate for an inaccurate assertion.

## What to reject

Reject guaranteed muscle gain, invented time savings, unsupported “everyone,” fake controversy, medical authority cues, and titles that the video never answers. YouTube’s misleading-metadata policy is a concrete boundary here. Negative language can win news clicks in studied settings, but that does not make shame a sensible brand strategy or establish long-term trust. [YouTube policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en), [negativity study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4)

Score the script on clarity, accurate promise, useful action, necessary qualification and a visible explanation. These are editorial checks, not a fabricated virality score. If the claim fails, rewrite it. If the graphic adds nothing, redesign it. If a sentence requires rushing, remove the secondary idea.
